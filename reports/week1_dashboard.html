The dataset at a glance

What we are working with

The complete CDC/NCHS Natality public-use files for 2018 to 2024, restricted to singleton births, with only the pre-delivery certificate fields we explore this week.

Source: CDC/NCHS Natality public-use files via the NBER mirror. Singletons only (multiples are excluded by design because they are roughly 60% preterm). The overall singleton rate sits below the familiar 10.4% all-births headline exactly as expected.

One record is one birth

A handful of real rows

We always begin the way a real analysis session does: load the table and look at actual rows. These eight are a random, reproducible sample of the raw fields (coded values mapped to readable labels).

Only these eight illustrative rows are shipped with the dashboard; everything else on this page is an aggregate, never raw microdata.

Before trusting anything

Data quality: missingness and sentinels

Missingness in administrative data is never random, so we map it first. Then we hunt the NCHS "not stated" sentinel codes that masquerade as real numbers (a BMI of 99.9, 98 cigarettes a day).

The discipline. Marital status is missing for about 10% of rows, correlated with reporting jurisdiction (missing-not-at-random); everything else is under 2%. Sentinel values get excluded from quantitative summaries, but genuinely rare-but-real values (a 13-year-old mother, a mother with 12 prior births) are kept, because they exist and are clinically high-risk.
Univariate exploration

Who is in the data?

Before relating anything to the outcome, we look at the cast of characters: the distributions of age, pre-pregnancy BMI, education, and payment source.

All as expected. Age is bell-shaped near 29, BMI is right-skewed with a real obesity tail (sentinels removed), education peaks at high-school-graduate and bachelor levels, and payment splits between private insurance and Medicaid. Nothing preterm-specific yet, which is exactly what a clean first look should show.
Bivariate exploration

What actually predicts preterm birth?

For each candidate predictor we compute the preterm rate within each level and let the gradient speak. The strongest legitimate signals are clinical.

Clinical history dominates. A prior preterm birth more than triples the rate (about a 3.4x risk ratio), summed pre-pregnancy risk flags show a clean dose-response climbing toward about 61% at four flags, and smoking adds roughly four points. Each is a group-level gradient, not a sharp individual separator, which foreshadows a modest modeling ceiling.
The finding we did not go looking for

The racial disparity, and whether education closes it

Preterm rate by race shows a roughly 4.7-point gap between NH Black and NH White mothers. A two-proportion z-test gives z about 280 (p indistinguishable from zero, and that huge z is mostly the millions of rows per group shrinking the standard error; the substantive number is the 4.7-point gap). The natural hypothesis is that it is really socioeconomic, so we try to falsify that by stratifying on education.

The hypothesis is falsified, and that is the finding. The race lines do not converge as education rises. A college-educated NH Black mother has a higher preterm rate than a NH White mother who never finished high school. Education, the supposed great equalizer, does not equalize this. Throughout, race is treated as a marker of exposure to racism and structural barriers, not a biological cause.
The statistical habit of the week

Significance is not the same as importance

How strongly does each numeric field correlate with the target? Every one is weak: the strongest, prior preterm birth, reaches only about +0.13.

No dominant signal hides here. Across tens of millions of rows, a t-test will call a one-unit BMI difference "wildly significant," yet no clinician would act on it. Effect size, not the p-value, is what matters, and the effect sizes are all small. This strongly suggests the honest modeling ceiling will be modest, because the causes of preterm birth largely live outside what a birth certificate records.
Planned next steps

What this exploration sets up

This week is acquisition, exploration, quality auditing, EDA, and visualization. The EDA is exactly what tells us how to do the following work well. None of it is done yet; it is the plan for the coming weeks.

1

Leakage-safe feature engineering

A tested gate that bars at-delivery fields (birthweight, Apgar, NICU), which are near-perfect but useless predictors. Given how weak the legitimate signals are, this is the most important decision ahead.

Planned
2

Calibrated baseline models

Logistic regression and tree models judged on rate-aware metrics and calibration rather than accuracy, given the class imbalance. A modest ceiling is expected and will be reported honestly.

Planned
3

A formal fairness audit

Turn this week's disparity finding into rigorous metrics: demographic parity, equal opportunity, calibration by group, each with confidence intervals and significance tests.

Planned
4

Explainability & gap decomposition

Verify what any model leans on, then ask how much of the racial gap measured factors actually explain, with appropriate causal humility.

Planned
