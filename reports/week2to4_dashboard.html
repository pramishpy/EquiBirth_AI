Week 2 · the most important engineering decision

The leakage trap, measured

Birth certificates record many fields at delivery (birthweight, Apgar, labor interventions, total prenatal visits) that predict prematurity almost perfectly and are useless as predictors, because they do not exist when a prediction would be made. The whole project depends on keeping them out.

The lesson in one number. A model trained on the barred post-delivery fields alone scores an AUC near 0.92. It looks near-perfect and means nothing. An automated gate fails the build if a barred field ever sneaks in, so the honest model below is trustworthy by construction. Race is a marker of exposure to racism, not a biological cause, and nothing here is a clinical tool.
Week 3 · calibrated baselines

The honest performance ceiling

Trained only on legitimate pre-delivery fields and judged on rate-aware metrics rather than accuracy (a rare outcome makes accuracy meaningless), the models land right in the published administrative-data band, around AUC-ROC 0.65.

Modest, and that is the finding. The gap between the leaky model (about 0.92) and the honest model (about 0.65) is the point: individual prediction from birth records is modest, which tells us these data support population-level equity diagnosis, not individual risk scoring.
Week 3 · the metric that matters for equity

Calibration holds, in aggregate and across race

For an equity audit, calibration matters more than raw accuracy: when the model says 10% risk, the real rate should be about 10%. After isotonic recalibration the error is tiny, and it holds within each large race group, which is the green light the fairness audit needs.

An honest qualification. A low calibration error means the scores are trustworthy on average, not that the model stratifies individuals well: most predictions sit within a few points of the base rate. Both things are true, and both are stated.
Week 4 · boosting and the first fairness audit

The fairness result is not the one you would guess

Tuned gradient boosting barely moves the ceiling. At a single screening threshold, the model is not less sensitive for higher-risk groups (the naive worry), it is more sensitive. The cost appears as disparate impact: those groups are flagged more often.

A value judgment, not a verdict. Higher sensitivity for higher-risk groups with more frequent flagging reflects the genuinely higher preterm rate those groups face. The tension between disparate impact and disparate treatment is real and is examined, not resolved, here. This is one model at one operating point.
